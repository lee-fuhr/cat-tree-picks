Cat Tree questions answered

Updated June 2026Independently researchedNo paid placement.

Quick answer

The right cat tree comes down to your cat’s size, age, and habits, plus the space you have and your budget. Focus on stability first, then materials like sturdy sisal and solid wood, and choose a height that matches your cat’s confidence level. A well-chosen tree keeps your cat entertained, exercised, and off your furniture.

How tall should a cat tree be?

There’s no one-size-fits-all answer, but most cats love a vantage point that’s at least as tall as they can stretch upright. A tree that reaches near the ceiling gives confident climbers a throne-like view, while a shorter model (hip-height to the average adult) works better for kittens, seniors, or cats that prefer to stay low. Consider your ceiling height and the cat tree’s footprint. Very tall trees need a wide, heavy base and sturdy construction to avoid tipping when your cat leaps onto the top perch. If you have multiple cats, a tall tree with multiple perches at different heights helps reduce squabbling over territory.

What materials are best for cat trees?

The two most important materials are the covering and the frame. Natural sisal rope is the gold standard for scratching posts because it catches claws and sheds fibers in a way cats instinctively love. Avoid trees wrapped in low-pile carpet or cheap synthetic fleece, they tend to fray faster and don’t satisfy scratching urges as well. For the structural frame, solid wood or thick, high-density particleboard holds up far better than thin MDF or hollow cardboard tubes. Real wood trees are heavier and more expensive, but they also last years longer. Look for platforms covered in short-pile plush fabric that’s easy to vacuum or spot-clean, and avoid materials that snag your cat’s claws.

How do I make sure a cat tree is stable?

Stability starts at the base. The tree should sit level on your floor, wobbling from uneven legs or a too-small footprint is a red flag. Many quality trees include a large, heavy base board or have attachment points for additional floor anchors. If you have a tall tree, check that the central post is thick and the joints are reinforced with screws (not just staples). Once assembled, give it a firm push at the top. If it rocks, you can stabilize it by adding weight to the base (like a sandbag or heavy books) or by using a ceiling tension rod if the manufacturer offers one. Never rely on a cat tree that feels unsteady; a fall can injure your cat or damage your home.

Should I get a cat tree with a treehouse or perches?

It depends on your cat’s personality. Treehouses (enclosed cubbies) appeal to cats that like to hide, nap in darkness, or feel protected while they sleep. Perches, open platforms, are better for cats that want to watch the room, bask in sunlight, or keep an eye on other pets. Many cats use both, so a tree that offers at least one enclosed space and one open perch gives flexibility. For a shy cat, prioritize a hideaway with a small entrance. For an active or curious cat, go for wide, elevated perches with a clear sightline to doors and windows.

What cat tree features help with scratching?

The most effective scratching surfaces are vertical posts wrapped in thick sisal rope. Look for posts that are at least as tall as your cat’s full stretch, a minimum of about two feet for an average adult, and taller for large breeds like Maine coons. Horizontal scratching surfaces, like sisal-covered ramps or plank perches, suit cats that prefer to scratch while lying down. A good cat tree will have multiple scratching posts at different angles and heights around the tree, not just one token post. Also note that carpeted posts often turn into nap spots rather than scratching targets, so sisal is the better choice if your goal is to save your sofa.

How many cat trees do I need for multiple cats?

In multi-cat homes, the general rule is to have one more vertical territory than you have cats. That doesn’t always mean a separate tree, a single large tree with multiple perches, cubbies, and platforms can work if there are enough distinct spots that cats can use without crowding. Watch for conflict: if one cat blocks access or guards a favorite perch, you probably need another tree or a wall shelf elsewhere in the room. Place trees in different rooms or at opposite ends of a large room so each cat can claim a zone. Tall trees with several levels allow cats to pass each other without direct confrontation, which helps reduce territorial stress.

Frequently asked questions

Can a cat tree be too tall for a kitten?

Yes. Kittens have less coordination and can fall from high perches. Until they’re about six months old, it’s safer to use a low tree (under three feet) or a tree with platforms close together, so a misstep isn’t a big drop.

Do cat trees work for senior cats?

They can, but you need to choose carefully. Look for trees with low stepping platforms (no more than six to ten inches apart), gentle ramps instead of ladder rungs, and a cozy hideaway on the lowest level. Avoid very tall trees that require big vertical leaps.

Is it better to buy a cheap cat tree or invest more?

Cheap cat trees often use flimsy materials, thin posts, and small bases that wobble after a few months. Investing a little more for a tree with real wood or thick particleboard and generous sisal wrapping usually means it will last years, not weeks. Mid-range trees from respected brands give the best value for most homes.

How often should I replace a cat tree?

Replace a cat tree when the posts are so frayed they no longer catch claws, or when the platform fabric tears and exposes foam that could be ingested. Also replace if the frame becomes wobbly despite tightening screws. Most well-made trees last two to four years with moderate use.

Are there cat trees that attach to the ceiling?

Yes, some cat trees use tension rods that press against the ceiling to add stability. These are great for tall, narrow trees that would otherwise tip. However, they require a ceiling that can withstand pressure (avoid popcorn ceilings or old plaster) and may leave small marks. They’re not a replacement for a wide base.

Can I put a cat tree on carpet?

You can, but it can reduce stability. Many cat trees come with rubber feet or a wide base designed for hard floors. On thick carpet, the tree may sink or rock. You can place a flat, rigid board under the base or use carpet-friendly floor protector squares to create a stable foundation.

In shortA good cat tree is an investment in your cat’s happiness and your furniture’s survival. Focus on stability, quality materials, and a design that fits your cat’s age, personality, and home layout. There’s no single perfect tree for every cat, but with a little thought, you’ll find one that your feline friend will use every day.
