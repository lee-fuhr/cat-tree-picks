How to choose the right cat tree

Updated June 2026Independently researchedNo paid placement.

Quick answer

Choose a cat tree that matches your cat ’s climbing style, your home ’s layout, and your budget, stability and material quality matter more than height. Look for a sturdy base, real sisal rope (not just glued-on carpet), and platforms large enough for your cat to stretch out. Our 2026 guide breaks down the key features to compare so you can buy with confidence.

What makes a cat tree truly stable?

Stability is the single most important safety feature, especially for larger or more energetic cats. A tree anchored to a wall via a included strap or bracket is your safest bet, many models over about 4 feet tall offer a wall attachment kit. Even without anchoring, look for a base that is as wide as the tree is tall, or at least the lowest tier extends out significantly. Particleboard cores can be heavy but often feel sturdier than cheaper hollow tubes. Read owner reviews for phrases like “doesn ’t wobble when my cat jumps onto it”. that ’s a reliable real-world test.

Sisal vs. carpet vs. faux fur, which covering lasts?

Natural sisal rope is the gold standard for scratching surfaces because cats can really dig their claws in and it holds up for years. Avoid trees that use only short-pile carpet or glued-on felt, those shred quickly and cats often ignore them. Faux fur on platforms and condos adds comfort but attracts fur and can pill. A good mix: sisal-wrapped posts for the main vertical scratching areas, and plush fabric only on lounging spots. If the sisal is just wrapped around a thin cardboard tube, it will sag; check that the post underneath is solid wood or thick particleboard.

How tall should a cat tree be for a happy indoor cat?

Most cats love to perch up high to observe their territory, so a tree that reaches anywhere from 4 to 6 feet gives them a satisfying vertical escape. In homes with ceiling fans or low ceilings, a 5-foot tree is often the sweet spot. For kittens, senior cats, or cats with mobility issues, a shorter tree with multiple platforms close together (not more than a foot apart) is safer and more encouraging. Very tall trees (over 6 feet) require serious base width to prevent tipping, and they often need wall anchoring.

Which features matter most for multi‑cat homes?

Space and escape routes. Look for trees with at least two separate “condos” or cubbies, preferably on different levels, so one cat can hide while another lounges. Wide perches (at least 16 inches across) allow two cats to share a platform without conflict. Avoid designs with only one narrow ramp or ladder, cats prefer to leap up, so open platforms and branches work better. Also consider a tree with multiple sisal posts so that cats don ’t have to fight over the only good scratching spot.

What is the best material for the frame and posts?

Solid wood or thick particleboard (often called MDF or engineered wood) provides the weight and durability needed for a long‑lasting tree. The main support posts should be solid wood logs or very thick cardboard tubes (at least 3 inches in diameter) wrapped in sisal. Never buy a tree with posts made of thin cardboard or hollow plastic, they will bend or collapse within months. The platform boards should be at least half an inch thick to avoid sagging under a heavy cat. Felt or rubber feet on the bottom prevent scratches on your floor and add grip.

How do I choose a cat tree that fits my decor?

Cat trees no longer have to be an eyesore. Many brands now offer neutral beige, light gray, or wood‑tone finishes that blend with modern furniture. Faux fur colors like cream, charcoal, or even “brown bark” look less like a toy and more like a piece of pet furniture. The shape matters too: a slim, tall tree with a small footprint works in tight corners, while a wide, low tree can serve as a room divider. Replaceable cushion covers are a bonus for easy cleaning. Avoid trees with garish bright colors or logos unless you’re okay with a statement piece.

Frequently asked questions

Can I wash the cushions and fabric parts of a cat tree?

Check the manufacturer ’s care instructions. Many modern trees have removable cushion covers that can be machine‑washed on a gentle cycle and air‑dried. For foam pads that aren ’t removable, spot cleaning with a pet‑safe upholstery cleaner is the next best option.

How often should I replace a cat tree?

A well‑built tree can last 3 to 5 years with moderate use. Replace it when the sisal becomes frayed to the point where it no longer holds shape, when the base starts to wobble despite retightening, or if any platform shows cracking. If your cat loses interest in the tree, trying a new scratching texture or adding catnip may revive it.

Are expensive cat trees worth the money?

In our research, the biggest difference between budget and premium models is stability and material longevity. A tree that costs a few hundred dollars often uses thick solid posts and heavy bases that won ’t move, while very cheap trees may use thin particleboard that warps. If you have a single small cat and a low budget, a moderately priced tree can work, but for large or multiple cats, spending more on a robust design pays off over time.

My cat ignores the cat tree. What should I do?

Place the tree near a window with a bird feeder view, or rub catnip into the sisal posts. Sometimes moving the tree to a high‑traffic area (like the living room) helps. If your cat prefers horizontal surfaces, try a tree that includes a wide platform or hammock, some cats simply prefer lounging to climbing.

Is it safe to leave a cat tree without wall anchoring?

For trees under 4 feet tall, wall anchoring is optional as long as the base is wide and the cat isn ’t a heavy jumper. For any tree that reaches over 4 feet or has a top platform larger than the base, always use the provided wall strap or bracket. Kittens and hyper cats can easily topple a tall tree that isn ’t secured.

Can I assemble a cat tree by myself?

Most medium to large cat trees require at least two people for the final assembly steps, especially when lifting the top posts into place and tightening bolts. Plan for about 30 to 60 minutes of assembly time. If you’re purchasing a very heavy tree, check reviews for “tool‑free assembly” or “pre‑drilled holes” to make the job easier.

In shortA great cat tree is an investment in your cat ’s physical and mental health, not just a piece of furniture. Focus on stability, natural sisal, and a height that matches your space and your cat ’s personality. Read owner reviews carefully, especially comments about wobbling or warping. With the right choice, your cat will get years of joy, and your couch will get a break.
